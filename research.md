    <h1>Language structure, primary data, and low-resource AI</h1>
    <p class="lead">
      My research asks how carefully documented linguistic data can improve both
      our understanding of language and the technologies built for languages
      that remain underrepresented in modern AI.
    </p>

    <div class="section-head">
      <p class="kicker">Research program</p>
      <h2>Four connected research directions</h2>
      <p class="lead">
        My work connects documentary fieldwork, linguistic analysis, corpus development,
        and computational modeling. These areas are not separate projects; they form a
        common research program focused on how underdescribed languages can inform both
        linguistic theory and language technology.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="grid-2">

      <article class="card">
        <p class="kicker">01 · Speech Technology</p>
        <h3>Low-Resource Speech Technology</h3>

        <p>
          <strong>
            How can speech systems learn robustly from small, heterogeneous,
            and linguistically complex datasets?
          </strong>
        </p>

        <p>
          I build and evaluate automatic speech recognition systems for languages
          with limited transcribed speech resources. My current work investigates
          cross-lingual transfer, data-efficient adaptation, speech-style mismatch,
          naturalistic versus constrained speech, tonal contrasts, and the limits
          of multilingual pretraining.
        </p>

        <p>
          A central concern is <strong>ecological validity</strong>: models that perform
          well on carefully elicited or read speech may behave very differently on
          spontaneous documentary recordings. I therefore treat recording style,
          speaker variation, community variation, and linguistic structure as central
          experimental variables rather than noise to be removed.
        </p>

        <p class="kicker">02 · Model Evaluation</p>
        <h3>Linguistically Informed AI Evaluation</h3>

        <p>
          <strong>
            What do aggregate metrics hide about what speech and language models
            actually learn?
          </strong>
        </p>

        <p>
          Word Error Rate and Character Error Rate are useful, but they can collapse
          linguistically different error types into a single score. Our work develops
          and applies evaluation approaches that ask which phonological, tonal,
          morphological, and grammatical distinctions are preserved or lost by a model.
        </p>

        <p>
          This direction is represented by our recent AfricaNLP 2026 work on multilingual ASR
          for African languages, where phonological-feature and tone-sensitive evaluation
          reveals systematic model behavior that word-level accuracy alone cannot show.
        </p>

        <p class="kicker">03 · Grammar &amp; Variation</p>
        <h3>Corpus-Accountable Grammar &amp; Variation</h3>

        <p>
          <strong>
            How can grammatical description capture the variation that actually
            occurs in documentary corpora?
          </strong>
        </p>

        <p>
          I study tense, aspect, negation, grammatical tone, focus, movement, and
          clause structure in underdescribed African languages, especially Edoid
          languages.
        </p>

        <p>
          I am particularly interested in the relationship between targeted elicitation
          and naturalistic evidence, and in grammatical descriptions that remain
          accountable to patterns across speakers, communities, varieties, and
          discourse contexts.
        </p>

        <p>
          My work treats variation not as a defect in the dataset, but as an empirical
          fact with consequences for linguistic theory, documentation practice, and
          computational modeling.
        </p>

      <article class="card">
        <p class="kicker">04 · Research Infrastructure</p>
        <h3>Documentation-Informed Language Technology</h3>

        <p>
          <strong>
            How can documentary resources become computationally useful without
            stripping away linguistic richness?
          </strong>
        </p>

        <p>
          For many underdescribed languages, computational research cannot begin with
          a large downloaded benchmark. It begins with field relationships, recording
          decisions, transcription conventions, annotation, lexical analysis, and
          language-specific knowledge.
        </p>

        <p>
          I therefore work across the full pipeline: audiovisual documentation,
          transcription and annotation, corpus and lexicon development, grammatical
          analysis, speech-dataset creation, model training, and linguistic evaluation.
        </p>

        <p>
          This creates a feedback loop in which documentation informs technology,
          while model errors generate new questions about language structure,
          representation, and variation.
        </p>

<section class="section alt">

    <div class="section-head">
      <p class="kicker">Research principle</p>
      <h2>Documentation and technology should inform one another</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="callout">
      <strong>Language documentation should not end with an archive, and language
      technology should not begin with a downloaded dataset.</strong>
      <br><br>
      For underdescribed languages, high-quality technology depends on understanding
      how the data was collected, how the language is structured, how speakers and
      varieties differ, and which linguistic distinctions matter.
    </div>
  </div>

      <div>
        <p class="kicker">Empirical focus</p>
        <h2>Uneme as a research laboratory</h2>

        <p>
          Much of my current work centers on <strong>Uneme</strong>, an Edoid language
          of Nigeria, alongside comparative and grammatical research on related Edoid
          languages and computational evaluation involving Yorùbá.
        </p>

        <p>
          Uneme provides an unusually rich setting for connecting fieldwork,
          documentation, grammatical analysis, variation, speech technology,
          and model evaluation within a single research program.
        </p>

        <p>
          The goal, however, is broader than a single language: I use Uneme to develop
          methods and questions that can generalize to other underdescribed and
          low-resource languages.
        </p>
      </div>
